⛰️ Mount Everest & The Roof of the World

🏔️ The Roof of the World

Imagine standing on top of the world, where the sky looks so deep and blue it could be outer space, and the clouds float far below your feet. That is what it feels like on the summit of Mount Everest, the tallest mountain on our planet. Everest is part of the Himalaya, a chain of giants so huge it can be seen from space.

The Himalaya was born in one of the most amazing slow-motion crashes in history. About 50 million years ago, the giant landmass that is now India drifted north and smashed into Asia. Neither side would give way, so Earth's crust crumpled upward like a rug pushed against a wall — higher, higher, higher! That is why the Himalaya is still rising about 4 to 5 millimeters every year. Today, all fourteen of Earth's eight-thousanders stand in Asia — and ten of them crowd together in this neighborhood of the Himalaya and its wild neighbor, the Karakoram.

🚩 Earth's Highest Summit

Mount Everest towers 8,848.86 meters (29,032 feet) above sea level — so high that a passenger jet flying overhead looks like a tiny silver bird. The mountain straddles the border between Nepal and Tibet (China), and it has collected many names over the centuries. In Tibetan it is Chomolungma — the Goddess Mother of the World. In Nepali it is Sagarmatha — the Forehead of the Sky. Western mapmakers named it after a British surveyor, George Everest, who never even saw the mountain! Since 2020, Nepal and China officially share the same height: 8,848.86 meters.

The summit is like another planet. Winter temperatures can plunge past -60°C (-76°F), and roaring jet-stream winds can blow faster than 160 kilometers per hour. The air is so thin that every breath delivers only about one third of the oxygen you get at sea level. That is why the area above 8,000 meters is called the Death Zone.

🗺️ Meet the Giants Around Everest

Everest is the star of the show, but it is never alone. It belongs to a royal family of peaks that all tower above 8,000 meters. Together they form a jagged, snow-covered crown on top of Asia. Right next door — almost touching Everest — stands Lhotse (8,516 m), the fourth-highest peak on Earth and home to one of the steepest walls of ice and rock anywhere. Just a day's walk to the east, Makalu (8,485 m) rises as the fifth-highest summit, a near-perfect pyramid of granite. To the northwest, Cho Oyu (8,188 m) is the sixth-highest and is often called the "gentle" eight-thousander — though no ice-covered giant is ever truly gentle!

The famous peaks keep coming along the range. Far to the east, where Nepal meets India, looms Kangchenjunga (8,586 m) — the third-highest mountain on Earth. Far to the west, in the wild Karakoram range on the Pakistan–China border, towers K2 (8,611 m), the world's second-highest peak and one of the hardest climbs of all. Many mountaineers say K2 is even tougher than Everest, with steeper faces, more avalanches, and storms that appear without warning — it was not climbed in winter until 2021! The neighborhood also includes Dhaulagiri, Manaslu, Annapurna (famous as the most dangerous of all), and Nanga Parbat, nicknamed the "Killer Mountain."

🧗 The Urge to Conquer

For thousands of years, people looked up at these giant peaks and treated them as sacred. Not until the 1920s did climbers seriously dare to set foot on Everest. Danger followed every attempt — blizzards, avalanches, and the terrifying thin air. In 1924, mountaineer George Mallory and his young partner Andrew Irvine disappeared high on the mountain. Did they reach the top? Nobody knows. Mallory's body was only found in 1999, 75 years later! His famous answer to "Why climb Everest?" became legend: "Because it's there!"

On May 29, 1953, the dream finally came true. Edmund Hillary of New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa from Nepal, stood together on the summit of Everest with the whole world below them. It was a team effort — Sherpas had carried loads, fixed ropes, and guided the way for decades. Without them, Everest would never have been climbed.

After 1953, the pioneers kept rewriting the record books. In 1975, Junko Tabei of Japan became the first woman to reach the top. In 1978, Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler climbed Everest with no bottled oxygen — an achievement once thought impossible. Messner even returned to climb it alone in 1980.

Today, trying to climb the "Seven Summits" or all fourteen eight-thousanders has become the ultimate mountain-game for extreme adventurers. In spring, hundreds of climbers line up at Base Camp, and on the clearest days more than a hundred people can stand on Everest's summit. But more crowds mean more danger: "traffic jams" in the Death Zone make climbers wait in the cold, burning precious oxygen. And every success leaves behind trash and tents, which is why clean-up expeditions and "leave-no-trace" rules are now a big part of Everest life. The mountain keeps teaching the same humble lesson — you never truly conquer a mountain. You only visit its summit for a few precious minutes, if it lets you.

Did you know? Everest is the highest mountain above sea level — but not the tallest from base to top! That honor goes to Mauna Kea in Hawaii, which rises over 10,000 meters from the ocean floor. And Ecuador's Chimborazo is the point farthest from the center of Earth, thanks to the planet's bulging waistline. Plus, Everest is still growing about 4 millimeters every year as India keeps pushing into Asia!

Remember! Above 8,000 meters — the Death Zone — moving, thinking, and staying warm are incredibly hard. Climbers spend weeks going up and down to get used to the thin air, and Sherpas do some of the most dangerous work by fixing ropes and carrying gear. Respect for the mountain and for the people of the mountains is what makes every climb possible.

🧠 Comprehension Quiz

1. How was the Himalaya mountain range — and Mount Everest itself — formed?

⛰️ About 50 million years ago, the landmass of India crashed into Asia. Earth's crust crumpled upward like a pushed rug, raising the Himalaya. The range is still growing about 4–5 millimeters every year!

2. Who were the first people confirmed to reach the summit of Mount Everest, and when?

🏔️ Edmund Hillary of New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa from Nepal, on May 29, 1953. Their climb was a huge team effort supported by many Sherpas.

3. Why do many climbers say K2 is harder than Everest, even though it is not as high?

🗻 K2 has steeper faces, more avalanches, fiercer storms, and almost no chance of rescue. It is so dangerous that no one reached its top in winter until 2021, while Everest is climbed by hundreds of people every year.

4. What is the "Death Zone," and name two dangers climbers face there.

🧊 The Death Zone is the region above about 8,000 meters, where the air is so thin that the human body slowly shuts down. Dangers include freezing temperatures, winds over 160 km/h, deadly "traffic jams" that waste oxygen, altitude sickness, and frostbite.
